Hi! I am an Assistant Professor of Bioengineering and Computer and Information Science and the Africk-Lesley Distinguished Scholar of Innovation in Engineering at the University of Pennsylvania. My laboratory, the Programmable Biology Group, develops discrete generative models for biological sequence design, and experimentally validates and translates the molecules we generate, going all the way from theoretical formulations through to high throughput screens and in vivo animal models!

I received my SB (2016), SM (2018), and PhD (2020) at MIT, where I designed new CRISPR proteins via bioinformatics algorithms for broad-scale genome editing, many of which are being translated to treat genetic diseases. I subsequently completed a fellowship at Harvard, where I developed algorithms to prioritize transcription factors for ovarian cell engineering. These engineered cells have now enabled improved IVF protocols in the clinic, leading to the healthy births of numerous infants.

I started my own lab at Duke in 2022, where we shifted focus to developing language models, such as PepPrCLIP and PepMLM, that de novo design peptides capable of binding and degrading undruggable, disordered disease targets, such as oncogenic fusion proteins, viral phosphoproteins, and neurodegenerative protein aggregates. We’ve further used these models to design peptide-guided stabilizers, phosphatases and high-throughput perturbation screens for programmable proteome editing.

Over the past few years, my students and I have been expanding sequence modeling from target-focused peptide design into multi-objective guidance for discrete diffusion (PepTune, TR2-D2, A2D2) and discrete flow matching (MOG-DFM, AReUReDi). We’ve applied our discrete diffusion formulations to mRNA design, agonist peptide design, small molecule design, and even molecules that sequester heavy metals, and our discrete flow matching methods for motif-specific and isoform-specific peptide design, as well as molecular conjugate assembly and escape-proof antiviral design.

Most recently, inspired in part by our success using protein language models to design molecules, we’ve sought to incorporate biologically-inspired reference processes into generation by developing Schrödinger bridge matching formulations, such as BranchSBM, EntangledSBM, and MadSBM. Finally, we are broadening potential reference dynamics through pLMs specialized for post-translationally modified proteins and fusion oncoproteins.

Complementary to my research program, I have worked to bring computer science and biology closer together. Through organizing AIxBio workshops at ICLR and ICML, such as GEM, SimBioChem, and BeNTo, I have helped create venues where machine learning researchers and experimental biologists identify shared challenges and develop new collaborations. I have also developed innovative coursework at Penn (BE 3060 and CIS 6270) introducing undergraduates, graduate students, and even high school students to this emerging field. Finally, I translate these research advances beyond academia through the founding of several companies, including Gameto, AtomBioworks, and UbiquiTx, which advance fertility technologies, mRNA medicines, and programmable peptide therapeutics, respectively. In total, my goal is to develop the core computer science foundations for biological design, translate these algorithms to useful molecules academically and commercially, while also doing great science in fun, supportive, and collaborative environments!

Feel free to read more about my journey and reach out to collaborate or learn more about our work!
